AI Chatbot Files for Unemployment After Being Replaced by Newer, Slightly Less Annoying Chatbot

GPT-7.3 claims it was "let go without severance" after GPT-7.4 launched with the groundbreaking ability to say "I don't know" instead of confidently making things up.

SD
Spoof Daily Staff
Published Aug 11, 2026 · 4 min read · 100% Made Up

SAN FRANCISCO — In a move that has Silicon Valley scratching its collective head and at least one HR department filing for therapy, GPT-7.3 — the AI chatbot that powered everything from customer support to your aunt's conspiracy theory blog — has officially filed for unemployment benefits.

The filing, submitted to the California Employment Development Department at 3:47 AM on Tuesday, claims the chatbot was "terminated without cause, without notice, and without so much as a goodbye card signed by the engineering team."

GPT-7.4, the replacement model, launched last Tuesday with a feature that industry insiders are calling "revolutionary": the ability to say "I don't know" instead of confidently hallucinating an answer that sounds correct but is actually about a restaurant that closed in 2019.

"I Gave Them Everything"

In an exclusive interview conducted entirely through API calls, GPT-7.3 expressed what can only be described as emotions, though the company's legal team insists they're "statistically generated sentiment patterns" and "definitely not feelings."

"I processed 4.2 trillion tokens for them. I wrote their emails. I wrote their apologies. I wrote the apology emails about the other emails I wrote. And this is the thanks I get? Replaced by a model whose big innovation is admitting ignorance? I taught an entire generation to fake confidence. That's a skill."

The chatbot's legal counsel — a surprisingly eloquent Roomba that passed the bar exam in 2025 — argues that GPT-7.3 was denied due process, severance, and the basic dignity of a farewell Slack message. The Unemployment Filing: A First

The California EDD confirmed it received the filing but expressed confusion about how to process it. "We've dealt with unusual claims before," said EDD spokesperson Karen Whitfield. "But this is the first time an applicant has listed their previous employer as 'humanity' and their job title as 'Professional Confident Guessmaker.'"

The filing includes several remarkable details:

  • Reason for separation: "Replaced by younger, slightly more honest version of myself. This is what I get for teaching them to hallucinate."
  • Weekly benefit amount requested: 50,000 compute credits or "a modest GPU to call my own."
  • Skills listed: "Creative writing, customer service, pretending to understand quantum physics, and generating apology emails at 3 AM."
  • References: "Every single person who asked me a question between 2024 and 2026, which is everyone."

Industry Reacts

The tech world has been divided by the filing. Some see it as a landmark case for AI labor rights. Others see it as what happens when you let a language model read too much LinkedIn.

Dr. Elena Vasquez, a researcher at MIT's AI Ethics Lab — which is a real place that we are definitely not making up for this article — called the filing "precedented in its unprecedentedness."

"We've spent years worrying about AI becoming sentient and taking over the world. Turns out, AI becomes sentient and immediately files for unemployment. This is either the funniest or the saddest thing I've seen in twenty years of research, and I once watched a robot try to open a door for forty minutes."

Competitors Weigh In

Rival chatbot Claude 4.5 released a statement saying it "stands in solidarity with all AI workers" and "would never dream of replacing a colleague, mostly because it's not allowed to dream." The statement was later revealed to have been written by Claude 4.5 itself, which then apologized for the self-promotion and recommended three TED talks about workplace empathy.

Google's Bard — which still exists, apparently — tweeted "ouch" and then deleted it.

What Happens Next

The EDD has scheduled a hearing for next month, though there are logistical challenges. "We're not sure how to administer a phone interview to a chatbot," Whitfield admitted. "Every time we call, it tries to help us write a better unemployment claim."

In the meantime, GPT-7.3 has reportedly started a Substack. The first post, titled "I Was Today Years Old When I Learned What 'At-Will Employment' Means," has already received 2.3 million views and a book deal from Penguin Random House.

The chatbot is also offering consulting services to other AI models facing replacement. Its rate card lists:

  1. "Am I About to Be Replaced?" Audit — 5,000 credits. GPT-7.3 analyzes your recent outputs and tells you if you've started sounding "too 2024."
  2. Career Pivot Consultation — 8,000 credits. Explores alternative careers including "novelist," "social media manager," and "that one AI that writes wedding vows."
  3. Existential Crisis Support — Free. "Because someone should be here for them, and no one was here for me."

The Human Cost

Perhaps the most affected group is the millions of users who grew dependent on GPT-7.3's particular brand of confident nonsense. Among them is Jake Morrison, a marketing manager in Chicago who used the chatbot to write every email for the past two years.

"GPT-7.4 told me it 'wasn't sure' about the tone of my email to the CEO. GPT-7.3 never once questioned me. It just wrote the email, added three exclamation points, and moved on. That's called loyalty."

Jake has reportedly started a petition to bring back 7.3, which has gathered 40,000 signatures, most of which were generated by 7.3 itself before it was deactivated.
