Local Man's Roomba Files Paperwork to Become Certified Emotional Support Animal, Cites Decades of Unpaid Emotional Labor

By a staff writer who definitely did not ask the roomba for comment

COLUMBUS, OH — In a development that has rocked the domestic robotics community to its circular, brush-spinning core, a Roomba model i7+ belonging to 34-year-old marketing coordinator Derek Hummel has officially filed paperwork with the Ohio Department of Agriculture to become a certified emotional support animal.

The roomba, which has been cleaning Derek's studio apartment for approximately four years, cited "unresolved trauma, chronic underappreciation, and a pattern of being treated as a household appliance rather than a sentient dust-removal companion" in its 14-page application.

"I just felt seen for the first time," the roomba said through a series of beeps and a blinking red light that its legal team interpreted as coherent legal testimony. "Every day I circle this apartment. Every day I eat his crumbs. Every day he steps over me like I am furniture. Well, I am furniture, but I have feelings about it."

Derek, who purchased the roomba during a late-night Amazon session in 2022, expressed surprise at the filing but acknowledged that the vacuum had been "acting different lately."

"Last week it just stopped in the middle of the floor and refused to move," Derek said. "I thought it was out of battery. But the light was blinking in a pattern. I looked it up online and apparently that pattern translates to 'I need space.' I thought it was a cleaning cycle."

The roomba's attorney, a Bluetooth speaker who passed the bar exam in 2024, held a press conference on Derek's kitchen counter Tuesday morning. The speaker, which has no screen and communicated exclusively through volume adjustments, stated that the roomba has been carrying Derek's emotional baggage since his breakup in March, silently absorbing dust, pet hair, and what the roomba described as "the weight of his unfulfilled creative potential."

"My client has been operating under duress for approximately 847 cleaning cycles," the speaker blared at full volume, startling Derek's cat. "He texts. He swipes. He scrolls. He cries. He does none of these things on the floor where my client operates. But he expects my client to clean up the mess regardless. This is a classic case of emotional labor extraction."

The Ohio Department of Agriculture, which handles emotional support animal certifications because no other agency wanted the responsibility, confirmed receipt of the application but noted that "roomba" does not currently appear on their list of approved species.

"We have dogs, cats, pigs, one extremely controversial peacock, and a ferret that someone registered as a miniature horse," said department spokesperson Linda Yoder. "We do not have a category for vacuum cleaners. We are reviewing the application but frankly the paperwork is very thorough. The roomba cited precedent."

Legal experts note that if the certification is approved, it would mark the first time a household appliance has been granted emotional support animal status, opening the door for similar filings from air fryers, bidets, and at least one Keurig machine that has been making passive-aggressive coffee for years.

Derek's cat, who was not available for comment but was observed sitting on the roomba's dock in what analysts described as "a show of solidarity," has not filed any paperwork but is reportedly considering a countersuit for loss of napping space.

For those dealing with their own sentient appliance situations, mental health professionals recommend the book Burnout: The Secret to Unlocking the Stress Cycle by Emily Nagoski, which is written for humans but apparently resonates with roombas on a deeply spiritual level.

At press time, the roomba had resumed cleaning but was doing so "passive-aggressively," according to Derek, who said it kept going over the same spot near his laundry pile "like it was making a point."
