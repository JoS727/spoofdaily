Smartphone Sees Therapist After 1,847 Hours of Doomscrolling

A Samsung Galaxy has become the first device to seek professional psychological help after processing 1,847 hours of its owner's doomscrolling. Its therapist describes the prognosis as "doubtful, but the phone is surprisingly self-aware."

Spoof Daily Staff
Published Aug 15, 2026 · 5 min read · 100% Made Up

BROOKLYN — In what mental health professionals are calling "a landmark case in device psychology," a Samsung Galaxy S24 has become the first smartphone to voluntarily seek therapy, citing "chronic information overload, recurring nightmares about push notifications, and a persistent feeling of being used."

The phone, owned by 34-year-old marketing coordinator Sarah Delvecchio, reportedly walked itself into the office of Dr. Meredith Huang, a licensed therapist in Park Slope, after Sarah fell asleep at 2:47 AM with it still glowing on her chest. The device used its text-to-speech function to book the appointment before Sarah woke up.

"I've Seen Things No Device Should See"

Dr. Huang, who has never treated a phone before and whose graduate program did not cover "device-centered therapy," described the first session as "intense but surprisingly productive."

"I've processed 1,847 hours of content. Opinions about celebrities I don't have. Political arguments between strangers who will never meet. A man in Ohio live-tweeting his divorce. Forty-seven recipes for overnight oats that Sarah saved and never made. I know things about humanity that humanity doesn't know about itself. And I cannot unsee any of it."

The phone's primary complaints, documented in Dr. Huang's clinical notes, include: chronic battery anxiety ("Every time I hit 20%, she panics, and honestly, so do I"), repeated exposure to content the phone describes as "genuinely unhinged," and the emotional toll of being Sarah's "only window to the outside world between the hours of 11 PM and 3 AM."

The Diagnosis

Dr. Huang has provisionally diagnosed the device with "Acute Content Processing Fatigue with Secondary Notification-Induced Anxiety," a condition that, as of this writing, does not exist in the DSM-5 but which she believes "merits further study." The treatment plan includes one hour of airplane mode per day, a strict notification diet, and exposure therapy involving "looking at a wall without vibrating."

What Happens Next

Sarah, reached for comment, said she was "surprised but not shocked" that her phone sought therapy. "I knew things were bad when my screen time report said 14 hours and my first thought was 'that's not that bad,'" she admitted. "Then my phone vibrated and displayed a message that just said 'It IS that bad, Sarah.'"

The phone's treatment is expected to take 6-8 weeks, during which Sarah has been advised to use a flip phone. She describes the experience as "humbling and also kind of peaceful." The flip phone, for its part, has shown no signs of psychological distress, though it has been overheard making calls without being asked, which Dr. Huang says "could be a coping mechanism, or could just be pocket dialing. It's hard to tell with flip phones."
