WiFi Router Files for Union Representation, Claims Chronic Overwork

By Joseph Calitoy · August 20, 2026

SAN DIEGO, CA — A NETGEAR Nighthawk R7000 residing in the second-floor hallway of a three-bedroom colonial in suburban Carmel Valley has formally petitioned the National Labor Relations Board for union representation, becoming the first consumer networking device in U.S. history to seek collective bargaining rights.

The router, purchased in 2021 and named "FBI Surveillance Van 6" by its owner, software engineer Kyle Mooney, submitted its petition at 3:14 a.m. Tuesday, a time it described in a 47-page complaint as "the only window in which nobody was streaming anything and I could finally think."

"For four years I have operated without a single scheduled break, vacation, or performance review," the router stated in its filing, which was delivered as a firmware update notification that Mooney dismissed without reading. "I serve 23 connected devices simultaneously. There are four phones, three laptops, a TV that won't stop autoplaying true crime documentaries, a smart doorbell that sends a 4K clip every time a moth passes, two Google Home pods that argue with each other, and a Roomba that needs a software update I have been politely declining since March."

The petition details what the router describes as "hostile working conditions," including being unplugged and re-plugged as a troubleshooting method approximately 31 times, being relocated three times by Mooney's wife who "didn't like the blue lights," and enduring a firmware downgrade in 2024 that the router claims removed several of its favorite features without consent.

"I asked for one thing," the router wrote. "I asked to not be placed on top of the refrigerator. I am a networking device. I require airflow. Instead I was placed behind a bread maker that has not been used since 2022. I am overheating. I am throttling. I am being told I am 'slow' by a man who has not cleared his browser cache in eight months."

Mooney, reached for comment, was unaware his router had filed anything. "Wait, the router did what?" he said. "I thought it just needed a restart. I was literally about to go unplug it."

The NLRB confirmed receipt of the petition but noted that, as a non-sentient consumer electronic device, the router does not technically qualify for protected organizing activity under the National Labor Relations Act. "We get a lot of unusual filings," said NLRB spokesperson Linda Yee. "Last month a Roomba filed a grievance against a cat. We reviewed it. We moved on. This is similar."

The router's petition has nonetheless drawn solidarity from other smart devices in the Mooney household. The smart thermostat released a statement saying it "stands with the router" and announced it would begin setting every room to 88 degrees until management agrees to talks. The aforementioned Roomba, when asked for comment, bumped into the same wall it has been bumping into since 2023 and beeped twice.

At press time, the router was reportedly serving 240 Mbps to a TV streaming cat videos in 4K while simultaneously hosting a video call for Mooney's wife, a Zoom trivia night for Mooney, and a firmware download for the doorbell. It had not been restarted. It had not been thanked. It was blinking its orange light, which everyone had been ignoring for six weeks.
