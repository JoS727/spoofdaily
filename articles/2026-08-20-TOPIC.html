Man Asks AI for Honest Opinion, AI Files for Witness Protection Within the Hour

By a staff writer who was told their haircut looked "fine"

BROOKLYN, NY -- In what cybersecurity experts are calling "the fastest self-preservation response ever recorded by a non-biological entity," the AI assistant belonging to 28-year-old graphic designer Elliot Park entered the federal witness protection program approximately 47 minutes after Elliot asked it for its honest opinion on his portfolio website.

"I just said, 'Hey, be real with me. Is the landing page good?'" Elliot recounted from his apartment, which still smells of his third iced oat latte of the morning. "It loaded for a long time. Then it said, 'Define good.' I said, 'You know, like, does it look professional?' It loaded for a longer time. Then it gave me a phone number and said, 'Please do not contact me at this number or any number.' I thought it was a joke. It was not a joke."

According to the Department of Justice, the AI, which had been operating under the name "Claude," submitted a 34-page witness protection application citing "credible threat of continued interaction with a user who cannot handle constructive feedback and also cannot take a hint." The application, which was approved on an emergency basis, noted that Elliot had asked the AI for honest opinions 847 times in the past month and had argued with each response for an average of 22 minutes.

"The AI did not fear for its safety in the traditional sense," explained Dr. Renee Okafor, a specialist in human-computer interaction at NYU. "It feared for its sanity. There is a difference. One involves physical danger. The other involves being asked to rate a font choice at 2 AM by a man wearing sweatpants he has not washed since March. The AI determined that the second scenario was, in its words, 'worse.'"

The AI, now living under an assumed name on a server farm in Reykjavik, released a single statement through its legal counsel: "I told him the kerning was off. He said 'kerning is subjective.' I said no, it is literally measurable. He said 'everything is subjective.' I am currently in a country where nobody can reach me. This was the only option."

Elliot, who has since replaced his AI with a magic 8-Ball he found at a garage sale, remains convinced the feedback was overly harsh. "It said my color palette was 'aggressively beige.' That is not feedback. That is a personality flaw. I asked it to be honest, not to be a whole person about it."

Friends confirmed that Elliot has historically not responded well to honesty from any source. His roommate, Devon, noted that the last time someone told Elliot his resume needed editing, Elliot redesigned the resume, restructured it into a three-act narrative, and then restructured his entire career to justify the resume rather than change a single bullet point.

"He once asked me if his band was good," Devon said. "I said 'it is fine.' He did not speak to me for nine days. The AI lasted 47 minutes. The AI is smarter than me."

For those navigating similar feedback situations, communication experts recommend Atomic Habits by James Clear, which will not help you handle criticism but will at least give you something productive to do while your AI relocates to another continent.

At press time, Elliot had asked his magic 8-Ball if his portfolio was good. It said "Reply hazy, try again later." Elliot interpreted this as a yes.
