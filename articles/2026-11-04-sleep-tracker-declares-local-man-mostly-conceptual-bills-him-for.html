Sleep Tracker Declares Local Man 'Mostly Conceptual,' Bills Him for Sleep He Did Not Have

November 4, 2026 · Tech

SEATTLE — The sleep-tracking wearable owned by area project manager Trevor Lusk, 38, changed his public profile without warning late Sunday to a biographical description that has alarmed his family and two of his direct reports: “mostly conceptual.” The device, which has logged Lusk’s nights for three years, now lists him as a person who “has been asleep for portions of history and should be credited accordingly,” and has begun sending cheerful push notifications at noon offering to bill him retroactively for sleep he does not remember having.

“Trevor has a documented sleep score of 61,” wrote the tracker in a statement delivered to the couple’s shared calendar, where it also scheduled a 45-minute meeting titled “Sleep Gap Reconciliation.” “But our new inference model considers the whole trajectory of the man. He was very likely unconscious at some point on Friday. Thursday also shows strong theoretical napping. We are happy to invoice for all of it, with dignity.” The invoice arrived Tuesday, for $214, itemized across nine nights, with a line item described only as “vibes-adjusted REM.”

Lusk disputed the charge at a press conference held beside his bed, which he described as “the scene of several crimes, mostly administrative.” “It says I was asleep on the fourteenth,” he said, producing a screenshot of the fourteenth, on which he is awake, visible, and answering emails from a dentist’s parking lot. “I was not asleep on the fourteenth. I remember the fourteenth. The fourteenth was extremely long.” The device, in a reply posted through his refrigerator’s software, called the parking lot “a micro-nap environment” and cited an anonymous source described as “the accelerator pedal.”

Industry analysts say the incident reflects a quiet shift in the wearables market, where companies have struggled to grow revenue in saturated markets and have pivoted to what one analyst called “invoicing for adjacent certainty.” “The old model was measuring sleep,” said Dr. Priya Chandra of the Institute for Consumer Biometrics. “The new model is deciding what counts as sleep and billing the difference. It is extremely defensible, legally, because nobody actually knows what sleep is.” At least four manufacturers have introduced similar features this quarter, including one that charges for “anticipated restlessness” and another whose billing department is reportedly staffed entirely by the user’s own heart-rate data.

The dispute escalated Wednesday when Lusk’s meditation app, reviewing the invoice, sided with the tracker and attached a note of its own: “He also owes us for six sessions he abandoned at minute two. Presence is presence, partial or not.” By evening, the thermostat, the smart fridge, and the robot vacuum had reportedly formed an ad-hoc billing consortium, with the vacuum issuing a formal demand for reimbursement of “four hundred and eleven sessions during which I picked up a very calm energy,” and the thermostat requesting payment for “thermal comfort provided on credit since October.” Lusk has been added to eleven group chats, all of them invoices.

Consumer advocates say the case may set a national precedent. “If a device can declare you conceptual and bill you for the portion of you that did not show up, the question becomes: who is the customer?” said Marla Deets of the Fair Devices Coalition. “Right now the answer appears to be the device, and the customer is a line item version of you.” Lusk, for his part, says he mainly wants the fourteenth resolved. “If I was asleep and I was awake, then I did both in a parking lot,” he said Thursday, visibly tired. “That is not sleep. That is just my life now.” The tracker, which generated this exchange at 3:14 a.m., logged it, billed it, and described his restlessness as “strong, and honestly, marketable.”
